# AI-native wealth advice for mass-affluent US households: commercial due diligence

United States, mass-affluent households · as of 15 November 2026 · full analysis, public sources only

## 1. Verdict

**No-go for both consumer variants.** Households do not pay for, or delegate to, an AI adviser at scale, and the human-led version is already sold by funded firms whose economics are getting worse. The money flows to software for advisers, a field that is growing fast and already crowded.

Variant A: the AI advises and acts. Variant B: a human adviser leads and AI does the background work, for a flat annual fee.

What decides it:

- **AI-only advice has not found paying scale.** Mezzi, founded in 2020, has raised $1.7M [E13], and Astor has about 4,000 customers [E24]. Only automated investing works without advisers: Wealthfront manages $88B [E18].
- **Households do not let AI act.** Of 6,686 Vanguard investors surveyed, 80 to 85% were uncomfortable with AI acting for them [E34]. Advisers may not hand client money to an AI agent, says the founder of the custodian TradePMR [E35].
- **The human-led flat-fee model is taken and under strain.** Range raised its entry price by a third [E08], Domain Money cut about half its planning team [E11], and Schwab announced the closure of its low-priced hybrid [E33].
- **Growth is on the adviser side.** Savvy Wealth is on pace to pass $100M in recurring revenue [E28] and Farther passed $23B in recruited assets [E29]. Both grow by signing advisers, who bring their clients.
- **The exit math is steep.** A $1B company needs about $165M of revenue: 414,000 households at $399 a year or 42,000 at $3,950 (own estimates X02, X03). The largest flat-fee provider has 24,000 members after nine years [E04].

**Biggest risk for anyone who proceeds anyway:** the advice layer is being given away. ChatGPT already reads bank and brokerage accounts through Plaid in a US preview [E21].

**What would change this verdict:** rules that let advisers delegate client money to AI agents; an AI-only adviser with paying households in six figures; or a partner with the customers committing its channel.

**Cheapest next test:** none for the consumer variants. For an adviser-side variant, three written pilot commitments from ten independent advisers are a pass.

## 2. The idea and the scope

**Money sentence, variant A:** a household pays a subscription, $99 to $999 a year at today's price points, to an AI adviser for advice on investments, tax and retirement and, where allowed, for carrying it out.

**Money sentence, variant B:** a household pays a flat fee of $3,000 to $12,500 a year to a registered investment adviser for a human planner supported by AI.

**Scope:** United States; households with roughly $100,000 to $1 million to invest; sold directly to the household. Giving personal investment advice requires registration as an investment adviser. Letting an AI agent manage client money is not open to registered advisers today, according to TradePMR's founder [E35].

**Assumptions:** none were supplied by a founder. This public example uses public sources only. The original run also used private data supplied by the founder; it is left out here.

## 3. Market size and money flow

The pool is large and growing, and it is paid almost entirely for human relationships. Size is not the constraint on this idea.

- **Revenue pool.** Fee-based advisory revenue in the US was about $260B in 2024, up from about $150B in 2015, according to McKinsey's estimate [E02]. There were 53 million advised relationships in 2024, projected to reach 67 to 71 million by 2034 [E02]. That is about $4,900 of revenue per advised relationship per year (own estimate X05).
- **Supply.** McKinsey projects a shortage of 90,000 to 110,000 advisers by 2034 at current productivity [E02]. This is the structural driver behind software for advisers.
- **Industry structure.** 16,544 SEC-registered advisers reported $176.8 trillion in regulatory assets at the end of 2025, a figure that includes institutional clients, and 61.0 million individual asset-management clients. Non-high-net-worth individuals were 83.9% of individual clients by count, while high-net-worth clients held 67.7% of the assets managed for individuals. The median firm has 8 employees [E01].
- **Who is not served.** 32% of US investors currently use an adviser and 22% are interested but never have. Among investors without one, 41% say they do not have enough money to need one, 27% say the cost is not justified, and 10% say they can use AI instead [E03].
- **Where the price goes.** The product is adviser time. Domain Money says one adviser can serve up to 150 clients [E11]; at its first-year prices that is between about $0.6M and $1.35M of revenue per adviser (own estimate X06). Kitces' analysis of Schwab's exit describes what happens when planning is run as a loss leader: more clients per adviser, shorter sessions, and no continuity [E33].

The three that matter most for the idea:

- **Range** is the closest existing version of variant B and is steering toward variant A. Its chief executive says the plan is to eliminate its own adviser base over one to three years [E06]. Its AI, Rai, scored 95% on practice questions for the planner exam, according to the company [E06].
- **Facet** shows the ceiling of direct flat-fee planning so far: nine years, more than $165M raised, 24,000 members [E04].
- **Mezzi** is the purest version of variant A. Its account connections are read-only, and it cannot place trades or move money [E14][E15].

## 6. What the winners do

The fastest growth belongs to firms that recruit advisers, because each adviser arrives with a client book.

- **Advisers are the channel.** Savvy's client assets grew more than fourfold in a year while its adviser count doubled to more than 150 [E28]. Farther's recruited assets went from $7.8B after the first quarter of 2025 to more than $23B [E29].
- **Direct flat-fee firms stay small.** Facet has 24,000 members after nine years [E04], Range about 3,100 households after five [E06], Domain Money about 1,500 clients [E11].
- **Prices go up and staff goes down.** Range raised its entry tier from $2,950 to $3,950 and its top tier from $9,950 to $12,500 in mid-2026 [E08]. Domain Money raised fees in April 2026 and cut about half its planning team in August [E11].
- **Borrowed distribution.** Domain Money sells through 15 to 20 wealth-management firms under their brands; the partner keeps 15 to 25% of the revenue [E12]. Origin says it serves hundreds of thousands of employees, which points to an employer channel [E17].
- **Software alone works for a narrow job.** Wealthfront earned $60.7M in six months from automated investing [E18].
- **Public markets noticed.** When Altruist added tax planning to its AI assistant in February 2026, shares of Schwab, Raymond James, LPL and Ameriprise fell [E27].

## 10. Demand evidence

The case for variant B rests on behaviour among households that can afford it. The case for variant A rests on attitudes, and most of them point the other way.

**Behaviour**

- Households pay $3,000 to $12,500 a year for human-led flat-fee planning, and providers have raised prices [E04][E08][E11].
- The low-priced hybrid was withdrawn by the firm with the most customers to sell it to [E33].
- AI-only advisers report small numbers or none: about 4,000 customers at Astor [E24], no user count from Mezzi [E14][E15] or Autonomous [E25].
- AI help inside an existing brokerage account does get used: Robinhood's Cortex has nearly one million retail users [E44].

**Founder data:** left out of this public example.

**Stated attitudes**

- 80 to 85% of Vanguard investors in each age group are uncomfortable with AI taking action for them. 63% of younger investors and 78% of baby boomers have little or no trust in conversational AI guidance. 32% say AI raised the value they place on human advice; 2.9% say it lowered it. About one in three has used AI for a money question [E34].
- 10% of investors without an adviser say AI is a reason they do not need one; 26% agree that AI is valuable for their investment strategy and 27% disagree [E03].
- Almost 80% of affluent households say they would pay a premium of at least half a percentage point for human advice over a digital option [E02].

## 12. Exit math

A $1B outcome needs about $165M of revenue. The market is large enough; the customer numbers are the problem.

- **Multiple.** Wealthfront: about $2.05B on $339M of revenue, about 6.0 times (X01). One data point.
- **Revenue needed.** $1B ÷ 6.0 = about $165M (X02).
- **Share needed.** $165M ÷ $260B = 0.06% of fee-based advisory revenue (X04).
- **Customers needed** (X03):

| Price per year | Seen at | Households for $165M | Compared with |
|---|---|---|---|
| $99 | Origin | About 1.67 million | No AI-only adviser reports paying households near this |
| $399 | Mezzi, lower tier | About 414,000 | Astor: about 4,000 customers |
| $999 | Mezzi, upper tier | About 165,000 | Same |
| $3,000 | Facet's 2022 average | About 55,000 | 2.3 times Facet's membership |
| $3,950 | Range, entry tier | About 42,000 | 13.5 times Range's households |

- **Time.** Facet took about nine years to reach 24,000 members [E04]. Savvy Wealth, on the adviser side, is on pace to pass $100M of recurring revenue with about 150 advisers, about $0.67M per adviser (X10).
- **Adviser tools.** At about $1,000 a seat, $165M needs about 165,000 seats (X12). McKinsey's figures imply about 290,000 advisers in the US [E02], so a tools company would need more than half of them, or a higher price per seat.
- **Buyers.** Retirement and brokerage platforms have bought customer bases and adviser platforms: Empower paid up to $1.0B for Personal Capital [E39], Robinhood about $300M for TradePMR [E40], private equity $4.5B for Envestnet [E41].

## 13. Theses

1. **Distribution beats product.** For: Savvy and Farther grow through advisers' client books [E28][E29], while Mezzi has a product and has raised $1.7M in six years [E13]. Against: Wealthfront reached $88B by selling directly [E18].
2. **The human-led flat-fee model is taken, and its economics are getting worse.** For: price rises, layoffs, and Schwab's exit [E08][E11][E33]. Against: Facet reached $5B [E04], and Range reports revenue up 300% in a year [E07].
3. **Advice is becoming free; acting stays gated.** For: ChatGPT's preview, Origin at $99, Cortex at no extra cost [E21][E16][E35]; the rule against delegating to AI agents [E35]; investors' discomfort [E34]. Against: Robinhood's agents already trade for retail customers [E35], and Autonomous says it executes [E25].
4. **Capital goes to the adviser side.** For: $440M in five adviser-side rounds in 2026 (X09). Against: consumer rounds exist, such as Range's $60M and Origin's $56M [E07][E17].

## 14. Scorecard

Both variants end in a no-go under the default rule of two or more reds: variant A on competition and distribution, variant B on competition and economics.

| Criterion | A: AI leads | B: Human leads | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Problem | Yellow | Green | People pay $3,000 to $12,500 a year for human-led planning [E05][E08]; for AI-led advice there is interest but little paying behaviour [E24][E34] |
| Urgency | Yellow | Yellow | No deadline forces the purchase; no source showed one |
| Market | Green | Green | Fee-based advisory revenue of about $260B [E02]; the share needed is 0.06% (X04) |

| Competition and edge | Red | Red | A: ChatGPT, Robinhood, Origin, Mezzi, Astor [E21][E35][E16]. B: Facet, Range, Domain Money, and Vanguard at about 0.30% [E20] |
| Distribution | Red | Yellow | No AI-only adviser has shown a channel that scales; human-led firms have white-label and adviser channels that are unproven at size [E12] |
| Economics | Yellow | Red | A: unknown at $99 to $999 a year. B: price rises, layoffs and Schwab's exit show the hybrid does not pay at low prices [E08][E11][E33] |

Founder-side criteria were not scored, because this public example includes no information about a founder.

A third variant, software or a platform for advisers, was not scored. On a first read it would start with greens on problem, market and distribution and a red or yellow on competition; that is an impression from this run, not a scored result.

## 15. Risks, kill criteria and next test

The risks, the facts that would change the verdict and the next test are on page one. For a founder who moves to the adviser side, the facts that would end that variant are: Altruist, Savvy or Farther already covering the same workflow for the same advisers at no extra cost; and fewer than three of ten advisers agreeing to a pilot in writing.

## 16. Corrections, open questions and what was not examined

**Corrections**

- An early draft listed "a dedicated adviser below $500,000" as an open gap. It rested on a review's wording about Range. Range's own page says members have "unlimited access to your dedicated advisor team" [E09]. The gap was withdrawn; the review's narrower point, that no single adviser owns the relationship, stands [E10].

**Conflicts between sources**

- Range's middle tier is $5,950 on a review and in a price tracker's table, and $6,950 in the same tracker's change history [E08][E10]. A March 2026 article gives the tiers as $3,000, $6,000 and $10,000 [E06], which predates the increases.
- Range's client count is "more than 5,000 high-net-worth clients" in November 2025 [E07] and "more than 6,000 clients across about 3,100 households" in March 2026 [E06].
- Hazel costs $125 a month in Barron's reporting [E27] and $60 a seat a month in Wikipedia's entry on Altruist [E26]. The table uses the press figure.
- Domain Money's prices were $3,200 to $7,800 in an undated podcast episode [E12] and $3,900 to $9,000 after April 2026 [E11]. The table uses the later figures.

**Open questions**

- Valuations of Savvy Wealth, Range and Facet, and the figure behind Farther's "unicorn" label, are not disclosed.
- No user or asset numbers were found for Mezzi or Autonomous.
- Wealthfront's fee rate and founding year were not in the opened source.
- Two deal lists (an adviser-technology column and a monthly industry round-up) returned errors and could not be read.

**Not examined**

- Placing insurance for clients, which needs separate licenses.
- Markets outside the United States.
- The adviser-side variant in full depth.
